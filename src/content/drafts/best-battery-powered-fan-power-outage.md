## What Matters in a Power Outage Fan

**Watt draw is everything.** During a power outage your battery is also running a refrigerator, phone charging, lights, and possibly medical equipment. Every watt the fan draws is a watt taken from something else.

**CFM per watt** — cubic feet per minute of airflow divided by watts consumed — is the metric that actually matters. A fan that moves 500 CFM at 45W is far more valuable than one that moves 600 CFM at 120W during an outage.

**Runtime on battery** — a fan drawing 15W on a 1,000Wh battery runs 55+ hours. A fan drawing 65W runs 14 hours. That difference is the gap between two nights of sleep and one.

**Noise** — you will be sleeping near this fan. Anything above 55 decibels at 6 feet becomes a problem by night 2.

### 4. Gazco Camping Fan with Battery — Best Standalone
**Draw: 5-15W | Runtime: 8-20 hours on built-in battery**

A camping fan with a built-in battery serves a different purpose than the others — it requires no solar generator at all. For someone who needs basic air movement and does not have a large battery setup, a standalone camping fan handles 1-2 nights without any external power.

The built-in battery fans I tested moved significantly less air than grid-powered fans at the same size, but for sleeping comfort at close range (within 3 feet) they work adequately.

**Price:** ~$40-60

### 5. Box Fan (Generic 20-inch) — What Not to Buy
**Draw: 100-120W | CFM: 2,500 | Runtime on 1,000Wh: ~8 hours**

Box fans move enormous amounts of air — 2,500 CFM dwarfs everything else on this list. But at 100-120W they drain a 1,000Wh battery in 8 hours.

During a power outage when you also need to power a refrigerator (30-100W), phone charging (15-30W), and lights (20-40W), a box fan consumes a disproportionate share of your capacity.

Box fans make sense for cross-ventilation at night — open windows, fan facing out to pull hot air through. For that specific use case at night for 2-3 hours, they work. As a primary cooling device running all day, they will drain your battery before you need it most.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the best battery powered fan for a power outage?
The Vornado 630 is the best overall fan for power outage use — it moves 545 CFM at only 55W, runs 16 hours on a 1,000Wh battery, and circulates an entire room rather than just one direction. For maximum runtime, the Honeywell HT-900 draws only 32W and runs 27 hours on the same battery.

### How long will a fan run on a solar generator?
A 40W tower fan runs approximately 22 hours on a 1,000Wh solar generator. A 55W fan runs 16 hours. A 100W box fan runs 8 hours. These estimates assume the fan is the only load — subtract runtime proportionally for other appliances running simultaneously.

### Can a solar generator run a fan all night?
Yes. Most solar generators can run a standard tower fan (40-55W) for 16-22+ hours on a single charge — more than enough for an overnight outage. A 500Wh generator runs a 40W fan for 11 hours. A 1,000Wh generator runs the same fan for 22 hours.

### What size generator do I need to run a fan?
A 500Wh solar generator is sufficient to run a standard tower fan overnight. For running a fan plus a refrigerator, CPAP machine, and phone charging simultaneously, a 1,000Wh unit like the Jackery Explorer 1000 V2 or EcoFlow DELTA 3 Plus is the right size.

### Is a box fan or tower fan better for power outages?
A tower fan is better for power outages. Box fans move more air (2,500 CFM vs 400-550 CFM) but draw 3x more power (100-120W vs 40-55W). During an outage when battery capacity is limited, a tower fan's efficiency advantage outweighs the raw airflow difference for most indoor cooling needs.

### How do I stay cool during a power outage without AC?
Combine three strategies: block heat entry with blackout curtains on south and west-facing windows (reduces heat gain 30-40%), run a low-watt tower fan (40-55W) for continuous airflow, and use cross-ventilation at night by opening windows on opposite sides of the house once outdoor temperature drops below indoor temperature.
