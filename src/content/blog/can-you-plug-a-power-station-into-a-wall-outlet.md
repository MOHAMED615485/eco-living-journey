**No. Do not plug a portable power station, or any generator, into a wall outlet to power your house wiring.** The Red Cross says: "Never try to power the house wiring by plugging the generator into a wall outlet." The practice is called backfeeding, and the Red Cross says it "puts utility workers, your neighbors and your household at risk of electrocution." The CPSC calls it "extremely dangerous."

This is the most important safety rule in backup power, and it is easy to break by accident when you are tired and the lights are out.

## ⚡ Quick Answer

- **Backfeeding means sending power the wrong way:** a cord with a plug on both ends takes power from your generator or battery into a wall outlet and into your home's wiring, and it can travel back toward the utility lines outside.
- **The risk is to other people:** the Red Cross and the CPSC both say it can electrocute utility workers and neighbors, and it puts your own household at risk too.
- **The safe options:** plug appliances directly into the power station, use a properly rated extension cord, or have an electrician install a transfer switch.
- **Our reading of the rule:** the Red Cross and CPSC wording is about generators. A battery power station has no exhaust, but its AC output can push power into house wiring in exactly the same way, so the same rule applies. Check your own unit's manual.

## 🚫 Why Backfeeding Is Dangerous

Utility crews who repair downed lines assume a de-energized line is safe. A generator or battery feeding your house wiring can send power back out through your service line, and the Red Cross and CPSC both say this can electrocute utility workers and neighbors. It can also overload the unit, overheat the cord or house wiring, or start a fire, and it can energize outlets in your home that are not meant to be live.

A cord with a plug on both ends (sometimes called a "suicide cord") is the usual way this happens. Do not make or use one.

## ✅ Safe Ways to Power Your Home Appliances

1. **Plug appliances directly into the power station.** This is the simplest and what the Red Cross recommends first: "Plug appliances directly into the generator." It works well for a fridge, a freezer, a router, phones, a CPAP and lights.
2. **Use a heavy-duty, outdoor-rated extension cord.** The Red Cross says the cord should be rated (in watts or amps) at least equal to the sum of the connected appliance loads. Check the whole cord for cuts or tears and make sure the plug has all three prongs, including the grounding pin.
3. **Have an electrician install a transfer switch.** The Red Cross says to have an electrician install a power transfer switch in accordance with the National Electrical Code. A transfer switch connects a power source to chosen circuits and disconnects your home from the utility while it is in use, so power cannot flow back out. Some power-station makers sell electrician-installed home-integration kits; follow the maker's instructions and local code.
4. **Consider a permanent stationary setup.** The Red Cross calls a permanently installed stationary generator the best option.

## 🔌 What You Can Plug In Directly

Most household devices run on 120 volts, and most major appliances with a special plug need 240 volts, which most portable power stations do not supply. Typical direct-plug loads are a refrigerator, a chest freezer, a router and modem, phones, a laptop, a TV, a CPAP and lamps. Always check each device's wattage against the unit's continuous and surge ratings. Our [surge vs running watts guide](/blog/surge-vs-running-watts/) shows how, and the [appliance wattage guide](/blog/what-appliances-can-solar-generator-run/) lists typical loads.

If you want to power an entire house, a portable unit is usually the wrong tool. See [solar generators for the whole house](/blog/solar-generator-for-whole-house/) and [power station vs whole-house battery](/blog/portable-power-station-vs-whole-house-battery/).

## 💨 If You Use a Gas Generator Instead

If you charge or power things with a gas generator, there is a second hazard: carbon monoxide. The Red Cross says to keep generators outdoors, away from doors, windows and vents that could allow carbon monoxide to come indoors, and that opening doors and windows or using fans will not prevent CO from building up indoors. The CPSC says to use them only outside and far away from windows, doors and vents, and to install battery-operated or battery-backup CO alarms and test the batteries monthly. A battery power station has no exhaust, which is one reason many people prefer one for indoor appliances. See our [power station vs gas generator comparison](/blog/portable-power-station-vs-gas-generator/).

## ✅ Bottom Line

Never connect a power station or generator to a wall outlet. Plug devices straight in, use a properly rated outdoor cord, or pay an electrician for a transfer switch. For the full outage plan, see [what to do during a power outage](/blog/what-to-do-during-power-outage/) and [what to do when power comes back on](/blog/what-to-do-when-power-comes-back-on/).

## ❓ Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I plug a power station into a wall outlet?
No. Plugging a generator or power station into a wall outlet to power your house wiring is called backfeeding. The Red Cross warns it puts utility workers, neighbors and your household at risk of electrocution, and the CPSC calls it extremely dangerous. Plug devices directly into the unit or have an electrician install a transfer switch.

### What is backfeeding?
Backfeeding is sending power from a generator or battery into your home's wiring through a wall outlet, usually with a cord that has a plug on both ends. The power can travel back toward the utility lines and endanger workers repairing them.

### Do I need a transfer switch for a portable power station?
Not if you plug appliances directly into the unit or use a properly rated extension cord. If you want to power circuits in your home wiring, the Red Cross says to have an electrician install a transfer switch in accordance with the National Electrical Code.

### What kind of extension cord should I use?
The Red Cross says to use a heavy-duty, outdoor-rated cord rated (in watts or amps) at least equal to the sum of the connected appliance loads, with no cuts or tears and a plug that has all three prongs including the grounding pin.

### Is a battery power station safer than a gas generator?
A battery power station produces no exhaust, so it does not create carbon monoxide the way a gas generator does. The backfeeding rule still applies to both. The CPSC says gas generators must be used only outdoors, far from windows, doors and vents.
